An estimator is a numerical quantity calculated on a sample that we expect to be a good approximation of a quantity with the same meaning in the population (the parameter).

  • For the mean of a population, “the best” estimator is the sample mean.
  • For the relative frequency of a category of a variable, “the best” estimator is the relative frequency in the sample.

An estimator, in contrast to an estimand, is a formula or algorithm used to estimate the target quantity from the clinical trial data, such as the difference in sample means between 2 treatment groups, or the Kaplan-Meier estimator of a survival curve. Statistical inference for an estimand requires a choice of the estimator and a measure of its precision. Typical methods of statistical inference are hypothesis tests, confidence intervals, and posterior credibility intervals in a Bayesian analysis. The estimate is the numeric value obtained when the estimator is applied to the actual data from the trial.

Population and sample — sampling error

From the population (parameters, unknown “?”) to the sample (estimates): the difference is the sampling error

Random sample

  • It is a random selection of subjects from a population
  • Technically “it is representative” of the population regardless of its size
  • In this sampling technique, each individual in the population is equally likely to be selected in the sample

Sampling variability.

  • The most complex part of the statistical process.
  • Fisher: the study of variability.
  • Variability due to sampling introduces a crucial concept: the standard error (SE).
  • This area of statistics opens the door to inference.

Simple sampling

  • Extracting a sample n from a population P is no easy task
  • Simple sampling (3 conditions):
    • n must be fixed
    • Subjects are randomly selected (all have the same probability of being selected) and this is independent
    • Subjects are extracted from an unchanged population
  • There are various sampling schemes: stratified, systematic, sequential

Simple sampling — human intervention

Always avoid human intervention in the selection of subjects, which would affect the inference process

“The mean” of the sampling means

The standard deviation of the sample means is the Standard Error of the Mean (SEM)

Sampling theory — SEM

Standard error of the mean (SEM)

\[SE(\bar{x}) = \sqrt{\frac{\sum(\bar{x}_i - \mu)^2}{N(n)}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\]

  • Very important concept in statistics
  • The greater the \(\sigma\), the larger the SEM
  • The higher the \(n\), the smaller the SEM
  • Precision depends on variability and \(n\)

The normal distribution

  • Organization of naturally appearing “population” data
    • Systolic blood pressure, body temperature, size, weight
  • It is characterized by two parameters: the mean (\(\mu\)) and the standard deviation (\(\sigma\))
  • \(N(\mu, \sigma)\) (centralization and variability)

The normal distribution — properties

  • Symmetrical; mean and median match
  • At a distance from the mean:
    • \(\mu \pm 1\,\sigma\) = 68% of the data
    • \(\mu \pm 1.96\,\sigma\) = 95% of the data
    • \(\mu \pm 2.5\,\sigma\) = 99% of the data

Why is the normal distribution important?

  • Although a random variable does not have a “normal” population distribution, the distribution of certain data extracted from random samples DOES have it!
  • Distributions of the sampling mean tend to be normally distributed even though the population data are not.
  • Distribution of the sample mean is the best estimator of the population mean

SEM and the 95% CI

The 95% confidence interval represents 2 SEM around the estimator (average)

95% confidence interval — example

If 100 random samples are drawn, in 95 of them the confidence interval will contain the parameter. In 5 it will not!

95% confidence interval — calculation

  • Lower limit \(= \bar{X} - 1.96\,(SEM) = 125 - 6.4 = 118.6\) mmHg
  • Upper limit \(= \bar{X} + 1.96\,(SEM) = 125 + 6.4 = 131.4\) mmHg

Mean 125 mmHg, 95% CI (118 – 131)

95% confidence interval

  • It is a measure of the dispersion of the estimate under study
  • Represents a range in which the actual population parameter is found 95% of the time (frequentist)
  • It is obtained from the standard error and can be calculated for any estimand: mean, proportions, OR, RR, r², etc.
  • More useful than the estimator alone because it evaluates “uncertainty”

95% confidence interval (cont.)

  • Provides a range of possible values for the parameter
  • The researcher will never be able to observe the real parameter
  • \(\bar{X}\) is the best estimate you can have from your sample
  • The 95% CI starts with this estimate and also provides the degree of uncertainty in this amount.

Finally: Standard deviation “versus” standard error

  • The term “standard deviation” refers to the variability of observations in a population or sample (reference ranges).

Hypothesis Testing

Test or hypothesis contrasts

  • A statistical hypothesis is a statement regarding some characteristic of a population. This hypothesis has not yet been tested.
    • The average age of patients in the emergency room is 30 years
    • The proportion of hospitalized male patients is 40%
    • Gender is associated with having type 2 diabetes

Test or hypothesis contrasts (cont.)

  • To contrast a hypothesis is to compare the statements with the reality we observe –in a sample–.
  • According to such a comparison, we will “accept or reject” our hypotheses.

Hypothesis

  • It is a statement about parameters of a population

Signification (p value)

  • The result obtained in my study disagrees more than tolerated a priori

Other examples,

  • Imagine a clinical trial testing a new antibiotic treatment for the management of severe central nervous system infections – very expensive
  • H0: Treatment in clinical trial has no effect in reality
  • H1: The treatment has a real effect

Other examples — types of error

Result of the study Reality: H0 true Reality: H0 false
No rejection of H0 Correct — the treatment has no effect and so it is decided. Type II error — the treatment does have an effect but we do not perceive it. Probability β
Reject H0 / Accept H1 Type I error — the treatment has no effect but it is decided that it does. Probability α Correct — the treatment has an effect and the study confirms it.

Types I and II errors

  • Type I (\(\alpha\)): \(P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ is true})\)
    • Level of significance = 0.05 (5%)
  • Type II (\(\beta\)): \(P(\text{accept } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ is false})\) (less bad)
    • \(1 - \beta\): \(P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ is false})\) – STATISTICAL POWER

For a given n, H1 and H0, if the value of α decreases the value of β increases, and vice versa.

Balance between α and β

  • For a fixed sample size, the two types of error cannot be reduced at the same time
  • To reduce β (or increase statistical power) you have to increase the sample size

Final summary,

  • Hypothesis contrast
    • Strategy for creating a decision (about a parameter)
    • Evaluates the probability of finding samples that differ even more than the one obtained
    • Provides a p value to be able to discriminate
    • Too much binary and recently very criticized

Final summary (cont.)

  • Confidence intervals
    • Quantifies the effect of interest (parameter) and the uncertainty of the estimate
    • Provides a range in which the parameter could be located
    • Allows to evaluate the clinical interest of the estimate
    • It should always be used –when possible–

Test for a single group — Z and t tests

Z test and t test

Either way, results are presented as a p value: what represents the evidence in my sample for rejecting H0

Example,

  • I think the SBP of the population is 120 mmHg! I select my sample,
  • \(H_0: \mu = 120\) ; \(H_1: \mu \neq 120\)
  • \(\bar{X} = 130\) mmHg
  • I apply one of the tests described (Z, t-test)
  • P-value = 0.01
  • There is enough evidence in my sample to reject H0

Example — confidence interval

  • \(\bar{X} = 130\) mmHg versus 120 mmHg
  • 95% CI \(= \bar{X} \pm 1.96\,(SEM)\)
  • 95% CI = [126 – 135]

The 95% confidence interval provides a range of values in which we are 95% certain that the true population mean lies. If the 95% confidence interval does not include the hypothesized value for the mean, μ0, we reject the null hypothesis at the 5% level.

Test to compare independent means

  • I have samples that come from two groups independent of each other
  • Cohort study, to study cardiovascular risk at a given site
  • I am interested to know if the BMI of men and women in my study is different (differs significantly)
    • Group of men in follow-up
    • Group of women in follow-up

Test to compare independent means — data

BMI SD n SEM
Men 25.53 2.44 74 0.28
Women 27.18 5.08 95 0.52

Independent means — hypotheses

  • \(H_0: \mu_H = \mu_M \;\Rightarrow\; H_0: \mu_H - \mu_M = 0\)
  • \(H_1: \mu_H \neq \mu_M \;\Rightarrow\; H_1: \mu_H - \mu_M \neq 0\)
  • \(\bar{X}_H - \bar{X}_M = 25.53 - 27.18 = \mathbf{-1.65}\)
  • \(p = 0.005\)

Test to compare independent means — 95% CI

\[SEM_{\bar{X}_1-\bar{X}_2} = \sqrt{(SEM_1)^2 + (SEM_2)^2}\]

\[SEM_{\bar{X}_H-\bar{X}_M} = \sqrt{(0.28_H)^2 + (0.52_M)^2} = 0.59\]

\[\text{95\% CI} = -1.65 \pm 1.96\,(SEM_{diff}) = -1.65 \pm 1.16 = [-2.81 \text{ to } -0.49]\]

Conclusion

  • There is a statistically significant difference in the BMI of men and women
  • Such a difference is: –1.65 kg/m², 95% CI [–2.81 to –0.49] (p = 0.005)
  • On average men have a BMI 1.65 kg/m² lower than women, but such a difference can be between –2.81 and –0.49
  • This CI does not contain zero, so it suggests a real difference at the population level in the BMI of men and women.

Analogous example: those on the low-carb diet lost more on average by 3.9 kg; after accounting for sampling variability this excess average loss over the low-fat diet group could be as small as 1.6 kg or as large as 6.2 kg.

This confidence interval does not include 0, suggesting a real population-level association between type of diet (low-carb or low-fat) and weight loss.

Non-parametric tests for means

  • Non-parametric statistics do not follow theoretical distributions of random variables
  • They do not require the conditions that parametric tests require.
  • They are based solely on the distribution of the data in the sample
  • They are also called distribution-free tests
  • Non-parametric tests have “less statistical power”

Mann-Whitney U test // Wilcoxon test

  • Wilcoxon rank sum test
  • Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon rank sum test
  • It is based on the median and ranks of the data
  • Not affected by extreme values
  • Requires access to the total data (dataset)
  • Its result is only in terms of p value
