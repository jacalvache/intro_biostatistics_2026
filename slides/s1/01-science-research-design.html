Why methodology matters

Training in statistics and methodology should discuss how studies are designed and how research questions are translated into study procedures, data collection processes, and analysis tools.

Research incentives — the problem

  • Research incentives focus on quantity, rather than methodological quality
  • Funders and journals prioritize novelty over incremental and replication research
  • Researchers’ agendas are dictated by short-term deadlines
  • Peer review remains unacknowledged
  • Methodological illiteracy is still accepted
  • Transparent and complete reporting remains rare

Practices resulting from prioritizing publication appearance over quality (1/2)

  • Poor study preparation and design
  • Data or analysis tweaking (e.g. p-hacking)
  • Incomplete reporting
  • Selective reporting

Practices resulting from prioritizing publication appearance over quality (2/2)

  • To use exaggerated claims when disseminating research results (over or wrong interpretation)
  • Publication bias
  • HARKing (hypothesizing after the results are known)
  • Reluctance to take corrective action post hoc

What is Science?

Oxford Dictionary definition: A branch of knowledge conducted on objective principles involving the systematized observation of and experiments with phenomena, especially concerned with the material and functions of the physical universe.

More simply: Science is the acquisition of reliable but not infallible knowledge of the real world, including explanations of phenomena.

Four terms: four meanings

  • Fact
  • Law
  • Hypothesis
  • Theory

A fact

(National Academy of Sciences)

In science, an observation that has been repeatedly confirmed and for all practical purposes is accepted as “true”.

Truth in science, however, is never final, and what is accepted as a fact today may be modified or even discarded tomorrow.

A law

(National Academy of Sciences)

In science, a descriptive generalization about how some aspect of the natural world behaves under specified circumstances.

e.g. the second law of thermodynamics

A scientific hypothesis

(National Academy of Sciences)

A tentative statement about the natural world leading to deductions or predictions that can be tested. If the deductions are verified, it becomes more probable that the hypothesis is correct.

If the deductions are incorrect, the original hypothesis is abandoned or modified.

A scientific hypothesis — testability

Research hypotheses must refer to concepts that can be studied scientifically (can be observed). “Living organisms are purposely designed by a maker”: no way to test for this hypothesis.

A theory

(National Academy of Sciences)

The goal of science.

In science, a well substantiated explanation of some aspect of the natural world that incorporates facts, laws, and tested hypotheses.

A key element of scientific theories is that they must be FALSIFIABLE (refutability).

If you examine 1 million swans and observe them all to be white, this does not prove the hypothesis that all swans are white — if you see one black swan, the hypothesis is falsified (falsification).

Evidence

  • Hypotheses are usually tested through experimentation
  • Two types of evidence accepted by scientists
    • Confirmation of hypotheses by data strengthens their validity
    • Repeated inconsistency of data with a hypothesis leads to rejection

Evidence — when a hypothesis is not verified

When a hypothesis is not verified, ask: is it because the hypothesis is incorrect, or because of problems in the statement of the hypothesis, or problems in the experiment? Repeat testing in many different ways.

Hypothetico-deductive reasoning

Karl Popper (1902–1994) proposed a reasoning cycle:

  1. Conception — a Problem, an Idea, a Previous observation, or an Existing theory leads to an Insight, which generates a General Hypothesis and then a Specific Hypothesis
  2. Assessment — the Specific Hypothesis is compared with new observations or experimental results
  3. The comparison leads to either Confirmation or Falsification of the hypothesis, which feeds back into the cycle

Example: Echinacea and the common cold

1. Observation

Individuals taking Echinacea say that it reduces the intensity and duration of a cold.

2. Hypothesis

Echinacea reduces the severity and duration of a cold (how is it defined).

Null: Echinacea has no effect on the duration or severity of the common cold.

Example: testable predictions

3. Testable predictions, clear outcomes

If Echinacea reduces the duration & severity, then individuals taking Echinacea should get sick less frequently and should recover more quickly.

4. Conduct an experiment

Example: conclusions

5. Draw conclusions

  • Cold symptoms lasted for the same amount of time in experimental and placebo groups
  • Echinacea had no effect on the duration or severity of the cold
  • Causally related

What next? Repeat the experiment using different variant cold viruses, use larger dose, other populations.

Failure of falsification

  • Null models not unique
  • Science versus no science based on falsification — it is a criteria of demarcation, not about the method
  • Should falsify the explanatory model, not the null
  • To build some amount of theory at some point, no statistical procedure is sufficient
  • It is called “confirmation” — models should be consistent with observed data

  • Users are using models (usually linear models)

Philosophy of Science — Inductivism

  • Inductivism: Empirism, concept developed by Hume (1777)
  • Causes of events can be determined by observation
  • Generalization from observations
  • Unlike deduction, the conclusions of inductive reasoning are probable given the evidence, in contrast to being certain

Philosophy of Science — Inductivism (cont.)

  • When events not following the rule occur, the theory becomes a probability rather than a certainty — this is where Bayesian statistics can play a huge role
  • Principle of the Uniformity of Nature: “The future will resemble the past”
  • Extend the ordinary logic (true/false) to continuous plausibility

Some forms of inductivism

Positivism (19th Century)

Science can rise above superstition by specializing in the description and analysis of observable phenomena, leading to discovery of natural laws.

Logical positivism

Science progresses toward truth by observation, formulation of hypotheses, empirical verification, leading to additional hypotheses. Scientific questions are referred to as “positive” while unscientific ones are “normative”.

Problems with inductivism

  • The theory chosen is not necessarily the right one
  • The observed data can be biased (quite common) — measurement error
  • Difficult to come up with an experiment without an underlying theory

In other words, we are born Bayesian…

Hypothetico-deductive and falsification — Fisher’s framing

Applies Popper’s theory to statistics:

“The null hypothesis is never proved or established, but is possibly disproved, in the course of experimentation. Every experiment may be said to exist only in order to give the facts a chance of disproving the null hypothesis.” (Fisher, 1947)

Issues with this theory

The hypothesis is true or false, there is no probability…

“Scientific hypotheses can be rejected (i.e. falsified), but never established or accepted the same way.”

The Bayesian approach

Refutation and hypothetico-deductive approaches were developed to avoid the concept that theories in sciences can be appraised in terms of their “probabilities”.

Theories usually lie between being certainly right or certainly wrong, we cannot tell…

The Bayesian approach — Poincaré

“It is often stated that one should experiment without preconceived ideas. This is simply impossible; not only would it make every experiment sterile, but even if we were ready to do so, we could not implement this principle. Everyone stands by his own conception of the world, which he cannot get rid of so easily.” (Poincaré, 1905)

The Bayesian approach — reasoning

Bayesian statistics have an inductive approach:

  1. Start with a prior distribution (prior knowledge, multitude of observations)
  2. Get data (observe under different conditions)
  3. Obtain a posterior distribution (update prior knowledge)

Frequentist vs Bayesian (1/3)

Frequentist Bayesian
Data A repeatable random sample — there is a frequency Fixed, probably a few samples
Belief No expression of belief (formally not present) → objective view on probability Approach deals with belief (formally present) → subjective view on probability; updates beliefs in the evidence of new data (posterior distribution)

Frequentist vs Bayesian (2/3)

Frequentist Bayesian
Estimate Point estimate using MLE and Least Squares Estimate Posterior distribution with high density interval (mean, mode, median)
Parameters Fixed and unknown Unknown, random, described probabilistically

Frequentist vs Bayesian (3/3)

Frequentist Bayesian
Interval Confidence interval: over an infinite sample size taken from population, 95% of these contain the true population value Credible interval: a 95% probability that the population value is within the limits of the interval
Hypothesis testing Statistical hypothesis testing with p-value and significance level Bayes factor — direct test of null and alternate hypothesis, measure of strength of evidence
Computation Less computationally intensive Computationally intensive

Cohort studies

Population is an aggregate of subjects (share a condition).

Closed cohorts

  • Group of subjects that are followed from a defined starting point to the occurrence of an outcome of interest
  • Cohort members do not change over time (except for mortality or loss of follow-up)
  • Individuals do not switch between exposure categories
  • Useful for short-term studies with high frequency of outcome

Open cohorts or Dynamic Population Study (DPS)

  • Simulate real populations
  • Participants can enter or leave the study without compromising study integrity
  • Subjects under study contribute with person-time of exposure

“Cohort” — the word’s origin

“Cohort” — what it means today

A Roman “cohort” (cohors) was one of ten divisions of a legion — the origin of the epidemiological term for a defined group followed over time.

Variations of cohort studies

Cohort studies — characteristics

Advantages

  • Allow to quantify absolute risk and RR
  • They follow the logic of the clinical question
  • Assess exposure effects on several outcomes
  • Useful in frequent outcomes

Disadvantages

  • Economic costs
  • Logistics costs
  • Extended time
  • May require large numbers of patients
  • Losses during follow-up

Example. Rotterdam Study

The Rotterdam Study is a prospective, population-based cohort study. The aim of the Rotterdam Study is to investigate factors that determine the occurrence of cardiovascular, neurological, ophthalmological, endocrinological, and psychiatric diseases in elderly people.

  • The study was established in 1990 by Prof. Albert Hofman of the department of Epidemiology & Biostatistics at the Erasmus Medical Center in Rotterdam, the Netherlands
  • Inhabitants of Ommoord, a suburb of Rotterdam, were invited to participate on a regular basis
  • The findings of the Rotterdam Study have been presented in close to 1,000 research articles and reports

Advantages

  • Efficiency in studying diseases (outcomes) or rare events
  • Short time
  • They have no follow-up

Cross sectional studies

Prevalence studies

A study that examines the relationship between a state of health-disease (exposure), other variables of interest that already exist, and one or more outcomes, in a population in a given time.

  • The exposure-outcome relationship is measured in terms of prevalence
  • Or according to the presence or absence of exposure in those who have or do not have the outcome

Cross sectional studies — schematic representation

Cross sectional studies — characteristics

Advantages

  • High control in subjects’ selection and measurements
  • There are no losses to follow-up
  • Absence of waiting time
  • Economic, fast studies
  • First step for further studies

Disadvantages

  • There is not a sequence of causality
  • Not useful in rare diseases
  • They do not determine incidence or absolute risk
  • Potential bias

Cross sectional — example

As part of a population-based screening program, researchers evaluated the prevalence of microalbuminuria in relatives of patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) compared with the general population. The prevalence of microalbuminuria was significantly greater in those with a family history of CKD than in the age- and sex-matched control group.

  • Prevalence was 9.5% in those with a family history of CKD, versus 1.4% in the age- and sex-matched control group with no family history of CKD

Observational designs — measures summary

Observational study design measures of disease, measures of risk, and temporality.

Activity

Researchers investigated whether pioglitazone was associated with an increased risk of bladder cancer in people with type 2 diabetes. Use of pioglitazone, an oral antidiabetic agent in the thiazolidinedione class, is controversial.

A cohort of 115,727 patients with type 2 diabetes was established, with patients entering the cohort if they had been newly treated with oral hypoglycaemic agents. Patients were considered exposed to pioglitazone if they had ever taken it, with duration and cumulative dosage recorded.

Activity — results

In the cohort, 376 cases of bladder cancer were diagnosed (at least one year after cohort entry, to account for latency). Each case was matched to as many as 20 controls on year of birth, year of cohort entry, sex, and duration of follow-up. A total of 6,699 controls were identified.

The researchers reported that the use of pioglitazone was associated with an increased risk of bladder cancer among people with type 2 diabetes.

Case series structure

  • By definition, it should sample subjects based on outcome or disease-associated status

A case series is a prevalence survey of a group of individuals with a particular disease, performed at a single point in time — particularly common for delineating the clinical picture of rare diseases. For example, sampling an outcome and describing its potential risk factors: this would be the case group of a case-control study.

Difference between the two designs

  • Cohort study: sampling is exposure-based, has a follow-up period, and the absolute risk of outcome can be directly calculated
  • Case series: only patients with the outcome are selected and do not allow the calculation of absolute risk — these may have an exposure, or this may not be taken into account

Key points

  • The number under study loses importance
    • A study of 5 patients with prosthetic reconstruction of the radial head is followed for 5 years and all remain with full functionality
    • Functionality estimation? Is it accurate? What confidence?
  • Selection biases or loss to follow-up may affect risk estimation
  • To investigate causality (etiology) or therapy almost always requires a control group (counterfactual thinking)

Randomized clinical trial

The clinical trial is a prospective human study that compares the effect of an intervention in a group with a control, in a disease or in a given condition.

Characteristics of clinical trials

  • They are experiments in humans
  • Studies in which the researcher establishes the population, assigns the intervention, and decides who and how to evaluate the outcomes
  • If well conducted, they provide high-quality causal evidence, making them the substrate of systematic reviews of the literature and Evidence-Based Medicine
  • They are not the only source of causal evidence, as they are not always feasible

Why modeling frameworks matter

  • Statistical modeling is a powerful tool for developing and testing theories by way of causal explanation, prediction, and description
  • In many disciplines there is near-exclusive use of statistical modeling for causal explanation and the assumption that models with high explanatory power are inherently of high predictive power

To Explain or to Predict?

  • Prediction
    • What will happen next?
  • Explanation
    • Why did it happen?

Explanatory modeling

  • A set of underlying (causal) factors measured by variables X are hypothesised to cause an underlying effect measured by variable Y
  • Focus: statistically testing the causal hypotheses
  • Data come from well designed experiments or observational studies

Predictive modeling

  • Goal: predict Y from new observations of given input variables X
  • This includes temporal forecasting: observations until time t (the input) are used to forecast future values at time t+k, k>0 (the output)
  • A predictive model is any model that produces predictions, regardless of its underlying modelling approach: Bayesian or Frequentist, Parametric or Non-Parametric, Data Mining Algorithm or Statistical Model, etc.

Two modeling paths

  • In each step, there are differences in the choice of methods, criteria, data, and information to consider when the goal is predictive vs explanatory
  • Conceptual / practical differences invariably lead to a difference between a final explanatory model and a predictive model
  • A priori determination of the main study goal is essential to conduct adequate modelling

Descriptive Modeling

  • Aimed at summarizing or representing the data structure in a compact manner
  • Unlike explanatory modeling, in descriptive modeling the reliance on an underlying causal theory is absent or incorporated in a less formal way
  • Unlike predictive modeling, descriptive modeling is not aimed at prediction

Descriptive Modeling (cont.)

  • Fitting a regression model can be descriptive if it is used for capturing the association between the dependent and independent variables rather than for causal inference or for prediction

Assumptions of causality (1/2)

  • Exchangeability (No Unmeasured Confounding). Conditional on the measured baseline covariates, the potential outcomes are independent of exposure assignment. That is, there are no unmeasured common causes of the exposure and outcome that would induce confounding.
  • Positivity. For every covariate pattern with positive probability in the target population, each exposure level has a non-zero probability of being observed. Thus, all relevant covariate strata contain individuals receiving each exposure level being compared.

Assumptions of causality (2/2)

  • Consistency. The exposure is sufficiently well defined such that the observed outcome under the exposure actually received corresponds to the corresponding potential outcome: this assumes that different versions of the same exposure do not produce different outcomes and that the exposure is measured without ambiguity.

Dark Causal Inference (1/2)

  • Causal reasoning is often implicit rather than explicitly stated, inferred from conclusions and recommendations rather than from clearly defined causal questions and assumptions.
  • Researchers may use ambiguous terms such as association, linked to, or risk factor despite drawing causal interpretations.

Dark Causal Inference (2/2)

  • This creates plausible deniability while retaining causal implications.
  • Approximately two-thirds of studies in major epidemiological and psychological journals address seemingly causal questions without clearly specifying the causal target or required assumptions.

“Effect” Without an Intervention — Electric Blankets and Childhood Cancer

  • An observational study described its objective and findings using explicit causal language, including “effect” and “effect estimate.”
  • However, no intervention was performed and the causal assumptions required for identification were not articulated.
  • Key lesson: Repeated use of causal terminology can transform an observational association into an apparently causal finding, creating “dark” causal inference.

The “Risk Factor” Label — Perinatal Exposures and Maternal Morbidity

  • Observational studies may describe exposures as “risk factors” or report “adjusted associations,” while drawing etiological or preventive implications.
  • This terminology can obscure the underlying causal estimand and identification assumptions, including exchangeability, positivity, and consistency.
  • Key lesson: A statistical predictor is not necessarily a causal determinant or an actionable intervention target.

The Convenience Disclaimer — Income and Happiness

  • Studies may frame the research question and interpretation in explicitly causal terms, while describing the analysis formally as cross-sectional associations.
  • A methodological disclaimer may state that causal-sounding terms are used merely for convenience, despite causal interpretations dominating the narrative.
  • Key lesson: A disclaimer cannot substitute for a clearly defined causal estimand and explicit identification strategy.

Hydroxychloroquine and COVID-19 mortality (1/2)

  • Early observational studies of hydroxychloroquine were interpreted as evidence of a therapeutic effect, despite substantial potential for confounding by disease severity and treatment selection.
    • In a large observational study, hydroxychloroquine use was associated with intubation or death (HR 1.04; 95% CI, 0.82–1.32), although the investigators appropriately emphasized the possibility of residual confounding.

Hydroxychloroquine and COVID-19 mortality (2/2)

  • Subsequent randomized evidence showed no mortality benefit, illustrating how an observational association can be misleading when interpreted as a causal effect.
  • Key lesson: Adjustment and propensity scores do not transform an observational association into a causal effect without credible identification assumptions.

COVID-19 severity: Smoking as a “Risk Factor” (1/2)

  • Observational studies identified smoking as a risk factor for COVID-19 severity, reporting elevated adjusted risks after multivariable regression.
    • The label risk factor can easily shift from describing a predictive association to implying that modifying the exposure would reduce disease risk.

COVID-19 severity: Smoking as a “Risk Factor” (2/2)

  • However, an adjusted association does not establish that smoking is a causal intervention target; this requires explicit causal assumptions and a well-defined intervention.
  • It was described as a modifiable factor.
  • Key lesson: A “risk factor” is not necessarily a causal factor—and statistical adjustment does not by itself establish intervention effects.

Research Questions: The Fundamental Triad

Research questions should be explicitly classified as:

  • Descriptive: Characterize distributions, patterns, or differences in health outcomes.
  • Predictive: Predict or classify outcomes using observed covariates.
  • Causal: Estimate how an exposure or intervention would change an outcome.

Research Questions: The Fundamental Triad — key principle

Key principle: The research objective should determine the estimand, methods, and variable selection—not the other way around.

The Problem with “Association”

  • Association is inherently ambiguous: different adjustment sets define different statistical associations.
  • If the adjustment set is selected during analysis, the research question becomes analysis-driven rather than prespecified.
  • Defining the target as an “association adjusted for X” does not allow meaningful assessment of unmeasured confounding.
  • For causal questions, use explicit causal language and prespecify the causal estimand.

The Problem with “risk factors to…” (1/2)

  • Terms such as risk factor, associated with, and linked to often conceal causal questions behind non-causal language, allowing causal interpretations without explicitly stating or defending the required assumptions.
    • The label risk factor does not define a specific estimand: the reported relationship may be crude, adjusted, or conditioned on different sets of covariates.

The Problem with “risk factors to…” (2/2)

  • Association does not imply interventionability: e.g., caviar consumption may be associated with wealth through socioeconomic status, but making people eat caviar would not make them wealthy.
  • Key message: A predictor or statistical “risk factor” should not automatically be interpreted as an appropriate target for intervention.

The “Plague” of Risk Factor Epidemiology & False Positives (1/2)

  • The Core Problem: Traditional risk factor epidemiology often focuses solely on estimating statistical associations without defining causal mechanisms, leading to a “plague” of false-positive research findings across nutrition, cancer, and environmental epidemiology.
  • Overconfidence in Statistical Models: Researchers frequently treat any statistical correlation as a causal “effect,” blurring the critical distinction between well-defined exposures (e.g., smoking) and vague, unmanipulable states (e.g., “unhappiness” or general BMI).

The “Plague” of Risk Factor Epidemiology & False Positives (2/2)

  • Public Health Disconnect: Uncritical tolerance for vague causal effects generates misleading conclusions that confuse the public and fail to inform actionable public health policies.

The “Risk Factor” Trap: Methodological Pitfalls (1/2)

  • Blind multivariable adjustment may lead researchers to interpret statistically significant coefficients as causal risk factors without a prespecified causal question.
  • Without a causal diagram (DAG), adjustment may:
    • block causal pathways by conditioning on mediators;
    • introduce spurious associations by conditioning on colliders.

The “Risk Factor” Trap: Methodological Pitfalls (2/2)

  • A variable associated with an outcome is not necessarily a causal determinant; modifying it may have no effect on disease occurrence.
  • Key message: Identifying actionable determinants requires a formal causal inference framework, not simply multivariable regression.

Transparent Use of Scientific Language — assumptions

Causal inference requires explicit consideration of exchangeability, positivity, and consistency, supported where appropriate by DAGs, target trial emulation, and sensitivity analyses.

Research Objectives and Interpretation

Objective Recommended language Example interpretation
Descriptive Describe, characterize, document, compare “The age-adjusted risk of dementia differed by 5% between the two educational groups.”
Predictive Predict, classify, forecast, risk stratification “The model predicted 5-year dementia risk with an AUC of 0.82.”
Causal Causal effect, intervention, impact, affect “Under the stated causal assumptions, vitamin B supplementation reduced 5-year dementia risk by 5 percentage points.”

Research Objectives and Interpretation — core principle

Core principle: The language of the paper should reflect the scientific question and the estimand—not merely the statistical model used.

The importance of language

  • Avoiding “causal” language with observational study designs is common publication practice, often justified as being a more cautious approach to interpretation
  • There is a substantial disconnect between causal implications used to link an exposure to an outcome and the action implications made
  • This undercuts common assumptions about what words are often considered non-causal and that policing them eliminates causal implications

What is Biostatistics

  • Statistics applied to biomedical problems
  • Decision making in the face of uncertainty or variability
  • Design and analysis of experiments; detective work in observational studies (in epidemiology, outcomes research, etc.)
  • Attempt to remove bias or find alternative explanations to those posited by researchers with vested interests

What is Biostatistics (cont.)

  • Experimental design, measurement, description, statistical graphics, data analysis, inference, prediction
  • To optimize its value, biostatistics needs to be fully integrated into biomedical research and we must recognize that experimental design and execution are all important.
